# Data Protection Impact Assessment — Kairos

**Status: DRAFT, unsigned. Not yet consulted on.** This document has no legal
effect until §6 is completed and signed by the controller.

**Assessment date:** 2026-11-08
**Assessed by:** engineering, from the code at commit `70e0130e`
**Processing assessed:** the Kairos church administration platform, as deployed
for the single-branch production pilot
**Follows:** the ICO's DPIA structure (describe → consult → necessity →
risks → measures → sign-off)

## Why this assessment is mandatory

Article 35(1) requires a DPIA where processing is likely to result in a high
risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, and Article 35(3)(b)
specifically names processing on a large scale of special categories of data.
The ICO's own screening criteria are met here on at least four counts:

- **special-category data** — health data about children (§1.3),
- **vulnerable data subjects** — children under 16, and adults subject to
  safeguarding or welfare concerns,
- **data about children** — a named ICO trigger in its own right,
- **new technology** and a new system processing data not previously held this
  way.

The scale is small at pilot, but "large scale" is not the only trigger and the
others are unambiguous. This is therefore a mandatory DPIA, not a voluntary one.

**It is being done at the right moment.** The pilot is one branch, on
production, with structural seeding only and **no member import**. So this
assessment describes an intended system rather than auditing a live one, and its
mitigations can be built rather than retrofitted. That window closes the day
real family data is entered.

## 1. Describe the processing

### 1.1 Nature

Kairos is an internal administration platform for Kharis Ministries, operating
as Kharis Church (§6). It is not a
public service and not a consumer product: every authenticated user is a member
of the church or someone acting on a member's behalf, and **under-16s cannot
sign in at all** (`apps/api/src/auth/service.ts:403-407`).

Technically: a Next.js web app on Cloudflare Pages, a Hono API on a Cloudflare
Worker, an Expo/React Native mobile app, and a PlanetScale Postgres database in
AWS eu-west-2 (London) fronted by Cloudflare Hyperdrive. Profile photos are
stored in Cloudflare Images. Transactional email goes through AWS SES in
eu-west-2. The architecture record is `../database-architecture.md`.

The data model is **55 tables** across 63 migrations. Collection is of three
kinds:

- **by the data subject** — sign-up, profile editing, forms they submit,
  express-interest flows, Gift Aid declarations;
- **by a leader about a member** — attendance marking, follow-up and pastoral
  notes, safeguarding records, health records for minors, role grants;
- **generated by the system** — audit events, notification events, request
  logs, and two daily cron jobs that reclassify members by age and attendance.

There is **no automated decision-making with legal or significant effects**, and
no profiling in the Article 22 sense. The two cron reclassifications
(`child → attendee` at 16, `visitor → attendee` after six attendances in 90
days — `apps/api/src/cron/lifecycle.ts`) change a label that affects which
screens show a record; they confer and remove no rights, and both notify a human
who can reverse them.

### 1.2 Scope — categories of data subject and of data

Full field-level inventory in `data-retention.md` §2. Summary:

| Data subjects | Notes |
|---|---|
| Members with accounts | Adults who signed up or were added by a leader |
| **Children under 16** | Cannot sign in. Records created by a parent or a safeguarding lead. |
| Attendees and visitors without accounts | Captured on welcome and first-timer forms |
| **Outreach contacts (`souls`)** | People who gave details at a street outreach and may have no further relationship with the church |
| Gift Aid declarants | May have no Kairos account at all — the public form predates enrolment |
| **People with no relationship to the church** | `audit_log.attempted_email` captures the address typed on a *failed* sign-in, including addresses matching no account |

That last row is easy to miss and belongs in a DPIA: the system holds personal
data about people who have never interacted with the church and have no way to
know a record exists.

Categories of personal data: identity and contact details; date of birth and
gender; home and secondary addresses; emergency contacts; profile photographs;
occupation; church-activity data (attendance, fellowship and department
membership, rota assignments, discipleship stage, mentor session notes); form
submission payloads across nine form types; pastoral, welfare and safeguarding
notes; Gift Aid declarations with declared name, address and the IP and user
agent of the declaration; consent records; and technical data (sign-in
timestamps, IP, user agent, country).

### 1.3 Special category data (Article 9)

**Structured, in exactly one table.** `member_health_records`
(`packages/database/src/schema/member-health-records.ts`), 1:1 with a member,
described in its own header as holding "safeguarding / health information for
minors (under-16)":

`medical_conditions`, `allergies`, `medications`, `dietary_needs`,
`additional_notes`, plus three nullable consent flags (`photo_media_consent`,
`medical_treatment_consent`, `data_processing_consent`) with a recorder and date.

There are **no columns anywhere** for ethnicity, race, disability, sexual
orientation, marital status or criminal convictions. Disability data, if
recorded, lands in the free-text health columns.

**Unstructured, and this is the gap.** `form_submissions.payload` is untyped
`jsonb` across nine form types. Religious belief beyond bare membership is
intrinsic to the altar-call, baptism and baby-dedication forms; the testimony
form has free text plus a category list including `Health/Healing`; the child
and guardian sections capture guardian names, phone numbers and relationships.
**None of the Article 9 access controls apply to it** — they attach to
`member_health_records`, and there is no field-level control over a jsonb
payload.

Safeguarding-adjacent data that is not strictly Article 9 but should be treated
as equally sensitive: `members.safeguarding_reviewed_at` / `_reviewed_by` /
`safeguarding_archive_decision`; and `member_followups.welfare_concern` /
`safeguarding_concern` with their free-text `notes`.

### 1.4 Scale

Being specific, because Article 35(3)(b) turns on it and because the trajectory
matters more than today's number:

| | Branches | Members (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| **Pilot (now)** | **1** | Structural seeding only. **No member import.** Members arrive as they sign up. |
| 2026 beta | 21 | — |
| 2033 target | **100** | **~20,000** (`../database-architecture.md` §1, §3) |

So the processing is *not* large-scale today and *is* planned to become
large-scale. A DPIA is required either way on the children's-data and
special-category triggers, and it should be **reviewed at each scale trigger**
in the architecture record, not only once.

Children are a minority of records but the highest-risk subset. The pilot gives
no basis to estimate the proportion; it should be recorded once real data exists.

### 1.5 Context

A church congregation is a community with pre-existing relationships, which cuts
both ways. Members may feel social pressure to supply data they would withhold
from a commercial service, and the people who can see a record are often people
the data subject knows personally. **Confidentiality inside the organisation is
therefore as important as security against outsiders**, which is why the
`admin_confidentiality` consent type exists and is required of privileged roles
(§3.4).

Data stays in the UK/EEA by design: PlanetScale in eu-west-2, SES in eu-west-2,
Cloudflare terminating in the UK.

### 1.6 Purposes

Taken from the published lawful-basis table and assessed in §3.

1. Administering membership, authentication, and contacting members about
   services and fellowships they belong to.
2. Recording attendance, tracking discipleship, co-ordinating departmental
   rotas.
3. Pastoral care, prayer follow-up, and **safeguarding of children and
   vulnerable adults**.
4. Optional newsletters, event invitations and outreach communications.
5. Claiming Gift Aid, including retaining declarations as HMRC evidence.
6. Protecting the platform against fraud and abuse, and auditing consent and
   administrative actions.

## 3. Necessity and proportionality

### 3.1 Is the processing necessary?

Yes for purposes 1–3, 5 and 6. A church with a safeguarding duty cannot
discharge it without knowing which children attend, who is responsible for them,
and what medical facts a leader must act on in an emergency. HMRC will not
accept a Gift Aid claim without the declaration. Authentication cannot work
without credentials.

Purpose 4 (optional communications) is **not** necessary, which is why it
correctly rests on consent and is the only consent type that is genuinely
optional.

The weakest necessity case is the `souls` outreach table — PII of people who
gave details once and may never return, retained indefinitely with no deletion
route. §4 treats this as a risk and `data-retention.md` carries the open
decision.

### 3.2 Lawful basis per purpose (Article 6)

Published at `apps/web/src/app/legal/privacy/page.tsx:134-210`:

| Purpose | Basis | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Membership administration, sign-in, contact | Legitimate interests | **No LIA exists** |
| 2 Attendance, discipleship, rotas | Legitimate interests | **No LIA exists** |
| 3 Pastoral care and safeguarding | Legitimate interests + legal obligation under safeguarding law | **No LIA exists** |
| 4 Optional newsletters and outreach | **Explicit consent** | Implemented and withdrawable |
| 5 Gift Aid | **Legal obligation** under UK tax law — expressly not consent | Reasoned through properly; the model for the others |
| 6 Fraud prevention and audit | Legitimate interests | **No LIA exists** |

Four of six purposes rest on legitimate interests and **none has a documented
balancing test**. Article 6(1)(f) requires one. This is an open decision in
`regulatory-mapping.md` §2 and a prerequisite for this DPIA being complete.

### 3.3 Article 9(2) condition per special-category purpose

**Currently recorded nowhere.** The notice describes the category sensitively
and names no condition. This is the single largest necessity-and-proportionality
gap, because without a condition the processing is prohibited outright.

> **DECISION NEEDED: adopt an Article 9(2) condition for each kind of special-category processing, and produce the DPA 2018 Schedule 1 appropriate policy document that two of them require.**
>
> **Recommended:**

### 3.4 Proportionality measures already built

An honest DPIA records what works, and several of these are better than typical
for an organisation of this size:

- **Capability-based access control, with one matcher.** `matchesCapability`
  (`packages/types/src/rbac.ts:177`) is the single implementation; the API, web
  and mobile each delegate to it with one call and no copies. Church-scoped
  grants reach everything, and **no branch grant ever satisfies a church check**
  — the asymmetry is enforced in the matcher, not assumed.
- **Grants re-resolved from the database on every request**
  (`apps/api/src/middleware/auth.ts:64`). The token's copy is never trusted for
  enforcement, so revoking authority takes effect on the next API call. There is
  no cached-permission window. This is the strongest data-minimisation and
  containment property in the system.
- **Deliberate separation of pastoral sight from administrative authority.** A
  `BranchAdmin` grant does **not** carry `safeguarding:read`, and
  `SafeguardingLead` does **not** carry `welfare:read`. Someone who administers
  a branch's data does not thereby get to read its safeguarding inbox.
- **Minor field redaction.** For a viewer without access, a minor's date of
  birth, email, phone, address, city, postcode and all three emergency-contact
  fields are nulled at the service boundary
  (`apps/api/src/members/service.ts:155-171`).
- **Health records restricted to two caller kinds**: the child's linked
  guardian (branch-independently), or a `SafeguardingLead` scoped to the
  **subject's** branch rather than the viewer's
  (`members/service.ts:1086-1106`).
- **A server-side consent gate** on every `/api/*` route, which verifies the JWT
  itself rather than relying on the router's middleware, explicitly so that
  "a technical user could [not] bypass the gate via `curl` after being made an
  admin and never accept the Confidentiality Undertaking"
  (`apps/api/src/middleware/consent-gate.ts`). Privileged roles must accept a
  Confidentiality and Data Handling Undertaking — effectively an Article 29
  control, and implemented.
- **Guardianship as a proper join table** (`member_guardians`, migration
  `0056`), with the previous single self-FK column **dropped** rather than kept,
  so "unguarded" means no live link and cannot disagree with itself.
- **argon2id at OWASP-2025 minimums**, constant-time comparison, per-hash
  parameter agility.
- **Pseudonymisation on erasure** rather than hard deletion, reasoned through
  against the foreign-key graph (`regulatory-mapping.md` §5).

### 3.5 Proportionality gaps

- **No retention enforcement anywhere** (`data-retention.md`). Indefinite
  retention is a proportionality failure independent of any security question,
  and it is the gap that applies to every data subject rather than a subset.
- **No ROPA** until `ropa.md` is reviewed and adopted.
- **No LIA** for four of six purposes (§3.2).
- **No Article 9(2) condition recorded** (§3.3).
- **The member directory's scope** was found during the review to be broader
  than necessary — whole member rows served where a projection sufficed. See
  §4 R3.

## 4. Risks to individuals

Scored ICO-style: **likelihood** (remote / possible / probable) ×
**severity** (minimal / significant / severe) → **overall** (low / medium /
high).

Severity here is weighted by the ICO's expectation that harm to a child, and
harm arising from safeguarding data, is severe almost by definition: the
consequences are not financial but physical safety, family relationships and
the willingness of a congregation to disclose anything at all in future.

**Every risk in R1–R4 is grounded in a defect that existed in this codebase
during the week of 2026-11-03.** They are not hypotheticals, which is why they
are scored on pre-remediation likelihood with the remediation state tracked
separately in §5.

### R1 — Unauthorised access to a child's health record via an unscoped capability check

**What.** `hasSafeguardingAccess` admits a `SafeguardingLead` grant. The review
found the bypass **was not scoped to the subject's own branch**, so a
safeguarding lead at branch A could read the health record of a child at branch
B — medical conditions, allergies, medications, free-text notes. Fixed by
`23093ec0` "scope the safeguarding bypass to the subject's own branch", with
`dd41c782` adding coverage for the branch-admin / guardian / safeguarding-lead
overlap.

**Harm.** Disclosure of a child's medical condition to someone with no lawful
reason, inside a community where the people involved know each other. Severe:
irreversible, affects a child, and would reasonably destroy a family's trust in
the church's handling of their data.

**Likelihood pre-remediation: possible.** It required a legitimate
`SafeguardingLead` grant, which is narrowly held — but it needed no
exploitation, only ordinary use of the UI by someone curious.

| Likelihood | Severity | **Overall** |
|---|---|---|
| Possible | Severe | **HIGH** |

### R2 — A member self-attaching as guardian to an existing child

**What.** The first-time-visitor form could write a `member_guardians` link
against a **pre-existing** member — resolved either from an explicit
`subjectMemberId` or from the phone-match safety net — rather than only against
a record the submission itself created. Because a guardian link grants
**branch-independent** access to the child's full record *and* their health
record, this is a self-service route from "can submit a form" to "can read a
named child's medical data". Fixed by `49058161` "link a guardian only to a
subject the submission created", which introduces an explicit
`SubjectOrigin` of `linked | matched | minted` and permits the guardian write
only for `minted`.

**Harm.** Severe, and worse than R1 in one respect: it is *targeted*. R1
exposes whoever happens to be at another branch; this lets a chosen child be
selected.

**Likelihood pre-remediation: possible.** Required no special grant — any user
who could submit a first-timer form.

| Likelihood | Severity | **Overall** |
|---|---|---|
| Possible | Severe | **HIGH** |

### R3 — Mass PII disclosure through an ungated or over-broad member directory

**What.** A cluster: the directory was not gated on any pastoral capability
(`fa4c88db`); whole member rows were served where a least-privilege field
projection was needed (`b77fca9a`, `8137ce5e`); the safeguarding surface was
conflated with the directory surface (`4cb6f2fe`); minors were not redacted in
the CSV export (`f005ef26`); the mobile screen and its nav row were gated
inconsistently (`7b7ac543`); and aggregate counts and cohort lists were
returned to members who could not act on them (`31b781e8`).

**Harm.** Disclosure of home addresses, dates of birth, phone numbers and
emergency contacts for an entire branch — including children's, before
`f005ef26`. Significant to severe depending on the subject; for anyone in the
congregation at risk from a former partner, severe.

**Likelihood pre-remediation: probable.** Needed only an ordinary member
session.

| Likelihood | Severity | **Overall** |
|---|---|---|
| Probable | Significant–severe | **HIGH** |

**Aggravating, and not yet remediated:** the audit log has no action type for
reading, changing or exporting a member record, and the CSV export routes write
no audit row. **If this were exploited, the church could not establish whose
data was taken** — which is precisely what Article 33(3) requires it to report.

### R4 — A session token signed with a repository-public key

**What.** `getAuthSecrets` (`apps/api/src/lib/auth-secrets.ts:43-44`)
substitutes hardcoded string literals committed to this repository when
`JWT_SECRET` or `JWT_REFRESH_SECRET` is unbound, **with no environment check of
any kind**. Because the signed payload is the whole `AuthContext`, and because
`matchesCapability` returns `true` unconditionally for
`systemRole === 'admin'`, anyone who can read the repository could mint an
administrative token for any member id. The same shape affects
`SELF_CHECK_IN_QR_SECRET` (`attendance/router.ts:72`), with far lower impact.

**Harm.** Total: every table, every branch, including all children's and health
data. Severe.

**Likelihood: remote, conditional on a specific operator error** — the secret
being unbound, misspelled or lost on a deploy. Note that no mechanism detects
this, and the repository contains no inventory of which Worker secrets are
actually bound (`key-management.md` §1), so the error would be silent.

| Likelihood | Severity | **Overall** |
|---|---|---|
| Remote | Severe | **MEDIUM–HIGH** |

**Not remediated.** This is being handled separately and is not on either PR in
§5.

### R5 — Permanent unauthenticated exposure of children's photographs

**What.** `requireSignedURLs` is set to `'false'`
(`apps/api/src/media/service.ts:41`), so every profile photo is served from
`imagedelivery.net/{accountHash}/{imageId}/public` with no authentication and no
expiry. The image id is a Cloudflare-generated UUID, so URLs are not
enumerable — but any leak is permanent and **there is no delete call anywhere
in the codebase**, so there is no revocation. The absence of a `Referrer-Policy`
header anywhere compounds it.

**Harm.** A child's photograph, permanently available to anyone who ever
obtained the URL, with no way to withdraw it — including after an erasure
request, which nulls `photo_url` and leaves the image in place, now
unfindable. Severe in a safeguarding context.

**Likelihood: possible.** Needs no attacker — a forwarded screenshot, a logged
URL, a referrer leak, or a CSV export passed onward.

| Likelihood | Severity | **Overall** |
|---|---|---|
| Possible | Severe | **HIGH** |

### R6 — Indefinite retention of everything

**What.** No retention enforcement exists for any data type
(`data-retention.md`). Compounding it, the privacy notice **publishes** periods
the system does not keep, including "sign-in logs … kept for up to 90 days,
then automatically deleted", which is false.

**Harm.** Every data subject is affected. Harm is diffuse rather than acute:
accumulating exposure, a larger blast radius for every other risk here, and —
for the outreach contact whose address is held for a decade after one
conversation — a loss of control that is exactly what Article 5(1)(e) exists to
prevent. The transparency failure is a separate Article 5(1)(a) harm.

| Likelihood | Severity | **Overall** |
|---|---|---|
| **Certain** (it is the current state) | Significant | **HIGH** |

### R7 — A temporary privilege grant that nothing expires

**What.** `member_roles` **has** an `end_date` column, but
`selectRoleGrants` (`apps/api/src/lib/grants.ts`) filters only on
`isActive` and **never reads `end_date`**. An administrator who sets an end date
intending time-limited authority gets a grant that stays live indefinitely. A
column that reads as an expiry and is not.

**Harm.** Elevated access — potentially `SafeguardingLead`, and therefore
children's health records — persisting long after the reason for it ended. This
is the scenario in the `incident-response.md` §7 tabletop, and it is the most
likely *real* incident for a church: no attacker, one unrevoked grant, and good
faith throughout.

| Likelihood | Severity | **Overall** |
|---|---|---|
| Probable | Significant–severe | **HIGH** |

### R8 — Inability to detect or scope a breach

**What.** No alerting of any kind. No rate limiting anywhere, so credential
stuffing is unlimited and unanswered. The audit log cannot answer a data-access
question (R3). **Request logs go no further than Cloudflare Workers Logs**,
which is enabled with head sampling (`observability.enabled: true`) and retains
a short window measured in days: `"logpush": true` appears **nowhere** in
`apps/api/wrangler.jsonc`, for either production or staging, and a Worker emits
to Logpush only when its own config carries that flag — so no account-level job
can be receiving anything. The Logpush-to-Axiom pipeline in
`../observability.md` is intended architecture that was never wired. Sentry is
named in the privacy notice as receiving error reports and **is also not wired
at all**. So the system has no log destination outside Cloudflare and no error
reporting whatsoever.

**Harm.** Not a primary harm but a multiplier on every risk above: a breach
that is detected late, or whose scope cannot be established, cannot be notified
accurately under Article 33 or communicated honestly to families under Article
34.

| Likelihood | Severity | **Overall** |
|---|---|---|
| Probable | Significant | **MEDIUM–HIGH** |

### R9 — Residual personal data surviving an erasure request

**What.** Account deletion is pseudonymisation, which is lawful and reasoned
(`regulatory-mapping.md` §5). But four categories survive unacknowledged:
`member_health_records` is **not scrubbed at all**;
`form_submissions.payload` retains free text including health and guardian
detail; the Cloudflare Images photo is not deleted; and **no audit entry records
the erasure itself**.

**Harm.** A data subject told their data is cleared, whose special-category
health data remains verbatim. Significant, and an Article 5(1)(a) harm on top
of the Article 17 one.

| Likelihood | Severity | **Overall** |
|---|---|---|
| **Certain** (it is the current behaviour) | Significant | **MEDIUM–HIGH** |

### Organisational measures

Already in place: branch protection on `main` that applies to administrators and
blocks force-push; a four-command CI gate that deploy workflows cannot skip; a
required Confidentiality and Data Handling Undertaking for privileged roles,
enforced server-side; mandatory authorization and branch-isolation tests per
module.

Newly documented, and taking effect on adoption: `incident-response.md`
(including the Article 33 procedure and the breach register Article 33(5)
requires), `key-management.md`, `backup-recovery.md`, `data-retention.md`,
`secure-sdlc.md`, `regulatory-mapping.md` and `ropa.md`.

Still absent: dependency scanning, secret scanning in CI, SAST, any penetration
test, and a tested database restore.

### The measures that gate the pilot

In the assessor's view, and offered as a recommendation rather than a decision:

1. **R4** — fail closed. A live vulnerability, not a missing control.
2. **R8, in part** — the two audit actions. Without them a breach cannot be
   scoped, and §6 cannot honestly conclude that detection is adequate.
3. **R1, R2, R3** — merged and deployed, not merely approved.
4. **R5** — because it is the one risk whose harm is *irreversible*: a leaked
   photo URL cannot be withdrawn, so the mitigation has no value applied later.
5. **The privacy notice corrections** — cheap, and the only item here that is
   currently telling families something untrue.

R6 and R7 are high but are reversible and bounded; they can follow the pilot on
a dated plan.
